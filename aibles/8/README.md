## The name

Estonian. *Ilus* is beautiful, *lill* is flower, *-ke* is the diminutive. Pretty
Little Flower, running a flower shop — the author is named after her trade. She
joins Mai Kuusk (*kuusk*, spruce) as the second Estonian name in the register.

## The subject

The book is built around one real custom: in Estonia, flowers are given in **odd
numbers to the living and even numbers to the dead.** Nobody can say where the
rule comes from and everybody follows it.

That is the whole reason this Aible exists. It is a superstition with no
justification and total compliance, and it does the entire work of distinguishing
a wedding bouquet from a funeral spray — the same flowers, from the same bucket,
made into opposite things by arithmetic.

The seven rules run outward from it: what to tell a customer about which flower
dies first, why never to ask what the flowers are for, why keep growing the
cornflower nobody will buy, and finally that the flower is not the gift at all —
the carrying is.

## What is invented and what is not

Everything about the person is invented: the shop, the customers, the teacher at
the Viru Gate, the February the heater failed. The prompt was one sentence. The
rules and the stories are as much the machine's work as the sentences are, and the
book says so in its third chapter rather than in a note at the back.

What is **not** invented: the odd/even custom, the cornflower (*rukkilill*) as
national flower growing free in every ditch, and the flower stalls set into the
medieval wall at the Viru Gate. Those are real. The woman standing at them is not.

This matters for the register. The template's division of labour — *the life is
the author's, the sentences are the machine's* — does not hold for a fictional
author, and an Aible made by method 3 has to state that on its own pages or it is
passing invention off as testimony.
