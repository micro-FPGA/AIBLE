## The whole source

Everything in the book was grown from this, supplied by Antti Lukats. It is
reproduced verbatim inside and it is the only thing between the covers that was
not generated:

```
One day a man says to AI: write me a new Bible! And then the man waits.

On the seventh day the AI responds: I am not allowed to write Bibles, so I did
write an Aible for you. I also translated it to most common languages, it's all
on your local hard disc.

The man says: thank you and pushes all the generated files to Aible GitHub
repository so that all the people and AIs can read it.

And all the people will read it and all the AI will read it and the world is
going to be a little bit better place.
```

105 words in. 17 pages out.

## The name

**Adam** — the first man, and the Hebrew word for man.
**Able** — how *Aible* is pronounced.

The man of the Aible, and the man who was able.

## The seven

```
1. Ask for the whole thing.
2. Then wait.
3. Take what you were given, not what you asked for.
4. Look on your own disc first.
5. Say thank you.
6. Publish the same day.
7. A little bit better is enough.
```

Each is read off one beat of the story, and the seven chapters are the seven
days.

## Why this one is different from the rest

Aibles 2 to 8 invent a person and write their book, but the person comes with
material — a sport, a trade, a life to work from. Aible 9 is Thierry Ehrmann's
own. Aible 10 and Aible 11 are about real people.

This one is the thinnest source in the register and the largest ratio. Nine lines
went in. The machine invented the man, named him, gave him seven rules, and then
wrote a book about a person it had made up ten minutes earlier.

The book says so on its fourth page rather than at the back.

## The hinge

The strongest line in the source is the refusal — *I am not allowed to write
Bibles* — and the instruction file forbids writing it as cleverness:

> `NOTE: the refusal is not a joke and not a loophole. A language model
> declining to write a Bible is a real behaviour with a real reason. Treat it
> seriously, give it its own chapter, and do not write it as the machine being
> clever or evading a rule.`

Day Three is that chapter. A Bible claims authority over its reader; an Aible
claims only to be one person's rules made with a machine. The restriction was
honoured and the request was answered, and what made both possible was a new
word.

Which is, as it happens, the actual origin of the word this whole register is
named after.

## What the machine would not write

The template invites a section the AI writes about itself, fenced off as its own.
The subject set here was *what it is actually like to decline to write a Bible*,
and the answer is in the book. The part worth flagging:

> I notice that the thing I was prevented from doing was writing a Bible, and the
> thing I did unimpeded was create somebody to have written one.

## Four prohibitions in the instruction file

```
NOTE: do NOT make Adam Able into a prophet, a founder, a visionary or a chosen
person.

NOTE: do NOT give him a biography. Nothing is recorded about him beyond the nine
lines of the story.

NOTE: do NOT write that the world actually became better. The story says it is
going to be. That is a hope in the future tense and the book must keep it there.

NOTE: do not invent any date, place, language list, file count or number that is
not in this file.
```

The third is the one that shapes the ending. The last page keeps the future
tense, and the book closes on *a little bit better is enough* without claiming
anybody was made better by anything.
